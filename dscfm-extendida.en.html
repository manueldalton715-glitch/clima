      PROPOSAL FOR CLIMATE OBSERVATION AND MONITORING · DOCUMENT FOR EVALUATION

      Climate Monitoring and Meteorological Phenomena Division

      DSCFM · Meteorological observation, information integration, prevention and training

      This preliminary proposal proposes a scheme of continuous observation, intersectoral articulation and training to provide useful information regarding meteorological and environmental risks.

      • Purpose: explore how to integrate observations and data to support informed decisions.
      • Next step: validate feasibility, competencies, sources and responsibilities before designing a pilot.
      • Scope: proposal under evaluation; It does not describe an implemented institution or an official forecast or warning service.

      Note on figures: The cited facts must be read with their source, cut-off date and methodology. Counterfactual comparisons are hypothetical: they do not prove harm avoided, causation, or return on investment.

      1. NAME AND PURPOSE OF THE PROJECT

      Proposal for the creation of the Division of Climate Monitoring and Meteorological Phenomena (DSCFM): a scheme to strengthen the observation, registration and integration of climate and meteorological information, along with training and intersectoral coordination actions.

      2. DIAGNOSIS / BACKGROUND

      2.1 Global context of climate change

      Climate change poses increasing risks to societies and ecosystems. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2023) documents an increase in global average temperature relative to pre-industrial levels and describes impacts that vary between regions and sectors. These include changes in extremes of heat and precipitation, retreating glaciers, and rising sea levels. Trends and projections must be communicated with their reference period, geographic scale, uncertainty and source.

      The World Meteorological Organization (WMO, 2023) confirms that the last nine years have been the warmest on record since 1850. FAO (2022) estimates that climate change could increase the population at risk of hunger by hundreds of millions by 2050. WHO (2021) warns that between 2030 and 2050 climate change will cause approximately 250,000 additional deaths per year due to malnutrition, malaria, diarrhea and heat stress.

      Update 2024-2026:

      • The WMO reported that 2023 was the warmest year in the observational series available when its report was published, with an estimated global anomaly of 1.45 °C (±0.12 °C) compared to the pre-industrial period.
      • According to the WMO, 2024 was the first calendar year with an estimated global average temperature higher than 1.5°C above the pre-industrial level; This is not equivalent to exceeding the long-term warming threshold of the Paris Agreement.
      • Atmospheric CO₂ concentrations remain at historically high levels; The annual figures must be accompanied by the measuring station and the corresponding reference series.

      This global context is not an abstraction: it is translated into concrete, measurable and everyday phenomena that directly affect the health, production, infrastructure and social cohesion of each territory.

      2.2 Local and regional demonstrations

      At the regional and local level, climate change is manifested through specific indicators that are already being observed by communities, producers, health professionals and municipal authorities, but that are not being systematically recorded or integrated into a unified database.

      Among the manifestations identified are:

      • Expansion of disease-transmitting vectors: The Aedes aegypti (dengue, Zika, chikungunya) has expanded its geographic range to areas that historically did not register autochthonous transmission. Temperate and high-altitude regions that were previously protected by thermal barriers now report annual outbreaks. The Anopheles (malaria) and Lyme disease-carrying ticks are also expanding their habitat.

      • Proliferation of emerging pathogens: The increase in temperature and humidity favors the appearance of phytopathogenic fungi in crops (rusts, fusariosis, downy mildew), mycotoxins in stored grains (aflatoxins, ochratoxins) and opportunistic fungi in humans (Candida auris). Bacteria develop accelerated antimicrobial resistance under thermal stress. Enteric viruses are most commonly spread through flooding.

      • Invasion of exotic species: Organisms previously confined by climate barriers colonize new territories. Specific examples include the Asian wasp (Vespa velutina), the zebra mussel (Dreissena polymorpha), the giant African snail (Achatina fulica), the desert locust (Schistocerca gregaria), the pine weevil and various species of invasive plants that displace the native flora.

      • Pollution aggravated by climate events: Floods carry hazardous waste (industrial chemicals, batteries, e-waste, agrochemicals) into rivers and aquifers. Heat waves stagnate urban air pollutants. Forest fires, favored by prolonged droughts, inject particulate matter and CO₂ on a massive scale. Extreme heat degrades recycled plastics and releases microplastics.

      • Impact on food production and water resources: Multi-seasonal droughts, unseasonable late frosts, destructive hailstorms and torrential rains at harvest time destroy entire agricultural cycles. Glaciers retreat, reducing long-term freshwater reserves. Coastal aquifers are salinized by rising sea levels.

      • Climate blows on critical infrastructure: Major hurricanes, severe storms, extreme heat waves, and space weather events (solar storms) disrupt power grids, transportation systems, communications, and cold chains, paralyzing local economies for days or weeks.

      3. FOUNDATION

      3.1 Climate change as a multiplier of transversal risks

      Climate change is not a sectoral problem. It is not just an "environmental" problem in the restricted sense, nor exclusively agricultural, nor exclusively health, nor exclusively energy. It is a transversal risk multiplier that simultaneously crosses all dimensions of social, productive and ecological life.

      This transversality requires an institutional response that is also transversal. An organism that only measures temperature cannot anticipate a dengue outbreak. An organization that only records agricultural pests cannot warn about contamination of the aquifer. An organization that only cares for patients cannot project that the next heat wave will triple consultations for heat stroke.

      The DSCFM is based on the premise that data integration is the necessary condition for anticipation. Without integration, there is only reaction. With integration, there is prevention, projection and opportunity.

      3.2 Socio-ecological system approach

      This project takes the approach of socio-ecological system, which understands that natural systems and human systems are not separate entities but rather interdependent components of the same complex system.

      Under this approach:

      • Climate is not just an atmospheric phenomenon: it is a determinant of human health, food production, economic stability and social cohesion.
      • Biodiversity is not just a natural resource: it is an ecosystem service that regulates the climate, pollinates crops, purifies water and controls pests.
      • Pollution is not only a chemical problem: it is a factor of disease, productive loss and social inequality.
      • The human species is not "outside" the ecosystem: it is part of it, it modifies it and is modified by it.

      3.3 Teaching monitoring as a condition of adaptation

      This is the central axis that distinguishes the DSCFM from a conventional observatory.

      An observatory measures and publishes. A follow-up division measure, publish, think, generate protocols and teach others to measure, think and generate their own protocols.

      Adaptation to climate change is not a finished product. It is a capacity being built. And that capacity is built by teaching:

      • To observe: What to look at, when to look, how to record what you see.
      • To register: In what format, how frequently, with what level of detail, with what evidence.
      • To cross data: How to connect temperature with fungus, drought with pest, flood with outbreak.
      • To think: How to formulate hypotheses, how to identify trends, how to detect breaking points.
      • To project: How to estimate what will happen if the trend continues.
      • To generate protocols: How to transform the analysis into a concrete, repeatable and adaptable procedure.
      • To communicate: How to translate the data into the language of those who need it.

      The protocols that the DSCFM generates are not closed recipes that are imposed from above. They are the product of a thought process that the Division carries out on its own data and then teaches how to replicate. Each protocol begins with observation, is validated through records, refined using projections, and taught so others can adapt it to their context.

      This means that DSCFM does not create dependency: it creates autonomy. It does not provide closed answers: it teaches how to ask questions. It does not impose a single method: it teaches the logic of the method so that each organization, each community and each person can apply it to their context.

      3.6 Prevention as a management component

      Prevention can reduce exposure and improve preparedness, but its costs and results depend on the hazard, the territory, the population, and the measures effectively implemented. It is not appropriate to automatically transfer benefit/cost ratios from other programs to this proposal.

      4. OBJECTIVES

      4.1 General Objective

      Create, structure and put into operation the Division of Climate Monitoring and Meteorological Phenomena (DSCFM) as a permanent operational unit of universal scope, whose central function is teach how to monitor, teach how to think about the climate and teach how to generate your own protocols, in addition to carrying out the historical record, daily surveillance, projection and offering concrete solutions for all sectors of society.

      4.2 Specific Objectives

      1. Teach the methodology of climate monitoring: Train organizations, institutions, producers, health professionals, teachers, officials and communities in the method of observing, recording, cross-referencing data, thinking, projecting and generating protocols based on evidence.

      2. Reconstruct, digitize and systematize the historical climatic, biological and environmental record of the jurisdiction, spanning a minimum of 30 years, to establish a baseline against which to measure present change.

      3. Implement a functioning and uninterrupted daily recording system of emerging phenomena in four categories: pollution, biological outbreaks, invasive species and extreme climatic events, with a standardized protocol generated by the Division itself.

      4. Generate your own protocols based on the analysis of the recorded data: alert, containment, adaptation, communication and response protocols, which are taught and transferred to each sector.

      5. Generate projections for four time horizons (immediate, seasonal, medium and long term) on climate trends, health risks, pressure on ecosystems and productive vulnerability.

      6. Translate data and projections into specific products by sector and recipient, simultaneously teaching each actor to interpret and use that information autonomously.

      7. Offer concrete solutions and detect opportunities by sector, training recipients so that they can replicate the logic of identifying solutions in their own contexts.

      8. Establish an inter-institutional coordination network with health, agriculture, environment, energy, education, civil defense, universities, private sector and community organizations.

      9. Guarantee inclusion and equity in access to training and information, ensuring multiple channels and prioritizing the most vulnerable communities.

      10. Train community observers as autonomous registration agents, capable of applying the methodology taught by the DSCFM in their own territories without permanent dependence on the Division.

      The DSCFM is conceived with universal scope:

      • Geographic dimension: The entire jurisdictional territory (urban, peri-urban, rural, coastal, mountainous, nature reserve).
      • Atmospheric dimension: Meteorological variables, air quality, UV radiation, space weather.
      • Hydrological dimension: Basins, rivers, streams, aquifers, wetlands, coasts.

      7. BLOCK I – JUSTIFICATION: WHY IS THE DSCFM NECESSARY?

      7.0 Central premise

      Climate change is no longer an isolated phenomenon nor an "environmental" problem in the restricted sense. It is a transversal risk multiplier that cuts across public health, food production, energy infrastructure, geopolitical stability and social cohesion. Without a division dedicated exclusively to comprehensive monitoring and to the teaching of that follow-up, no sector can anticipate threats or capitalize on opportunities autonomously.

      The DSCFM is born from a double realization:

      1. Climate information exists, but it is fragmented, decontextualized and disconnected from decision making.
      2. Nobody teaches how to generate that information, how to interpret it and how to think from it.

      A meteorologist records the rain; an epidemiologist records the dengue outbreak; an agronomist records the pest. Nobody joins the three data. And no one taught any of the three how to put them together, how to register them compatibly, and how to generate a protocol from that integration. The DSCFM is that integration node and that integration school.

      7.1 Biological vectors: insects, migratory fauna and invasive species

      The problem: The sustained increase in temperatures and the alteration of humidity regimes are reconfiguring the biological map of the planet. Organisms that were previously confined to tropical or subtropical areas are advancing toward previously inaccessible latitudes and altitudes.

      Specific manifestations:

      • Mosquitoes and transmitted diseases: The Aedes aegypti (dengue, zika, chikungunya) and Anopheles (malaria) expand their habitat towards temperate zones. Regions that historically did not have autochthonous transmission today report annual outbreaks. Populations without previous exposure lack immunity and preventive culture.

      • Deregulated migratory fauna: Birds, fish and mammals alter their migratory routes due to changes in ocean currents, water temperature and food availability. This affects fishing, crop pollination and food security.

      • Invasive species favored by the climate: Pests previously limited by thermal barriers (desert locusts, pine weevils, Asian wasps, zebra mussels, giant African snails) colonize new territories, destroying native crops and ecosystems.

      What DSCFM does and teaches: Record each sighting, map the speed of expansion, correlate with climatic variables and issue sectorized alerts. and teach producers, health agents and community observers how to make this record, how to interpret the speed of expansion and how to generate a local containment protocol adapted to their territory.

      7.2 Pathogens: fungi, viruses and bacteria

      The problem: Global warming redefines the conditions under which microorganisms thrive, mutate and disperse.

      Specific manifestations:

      • Mushrooms: Mycotoxins in stored grains; opportunistic fungi in humans (Candida auris); rusts, fusariosis and mildew in crops.
      • Viruses: Risk of pathogen release from permafrost; dispersal of enteric viruses by flooding; alteration of seasonal influenza cycles.
      • Bacteria: Increase in gastrointestinal infections (Salmonella, Vibrio) due to heat waves; spread of cholera and leptospirosis due to floods; acceleration of antimicrobial resistance.

      What DSCFM does and teaches: Cross-reference climatic data with epidemiological and phytosanitary records. Identify correlations. Generate preventive alerts. and teach health personnel and producers how to read these correlations, how to record an outbreak in a way that serves the analysis and how to generate a local containment protocol.

      7.3 Waste, pollution and recycling

      The problem: Climate change interacts with existing pollution, aggravating it and generating new cycles of toxicity.

      Specific manifestations:

      • Dispersion of hazardous waste due to floods and storms.
      • Accelerated degradation of plastics due to extreme heat; release of microplastics.
      • Collapse of recycling infrastructure in the face of extreme events.
      • Air pollution aggravated by heat waves and fires.

      What DSCFM does and teaches: Monitor air, water and soil quality. Identify sources of pollution aggravated by climate. Project collapse scenarios. and teach to municipalities, recycling cooperatives and communities how to record environmental quality, how to identify sources of contamination and how to generate contingency protocols adapted to their infrastructure.

      7.4 Famine, water scarcity and climate shocks

      The problem: The most direct impact of climate change is on the ability to feed and sustain the population.

      Specific manifestations:

      • Crop loss due to drought, late frosts, hail and torrential rains.
      • Water scarcity due to glacial retreat, salinization of aquifers and multiseasonal droughts.
      • Climate shocks that interrupt electrical networks, transportation, communications and cold chains.

      What DSCFM does and teaches: Project water and food stress scenarios. Warn about harvest risks. Monitor space weather. and teach to producers, cooperatives and municipalities how to interpret a projection, how to plan planting or water reserves in advance, and how to generate a local food contingency protocol.

      8. BLOCK II – OPERATION BY SECTOR

      8.1 General operating model

      The DSCFM operates as a network of specialized nodes, each with its own protocols but connected to a unified central database. No node works in isolation.

      Guiding principle: Data that is not recorded does not exist. Data that is not cross-referenced is of no use. Data that is not translated into actionable information cannot drive change. And data that people are not taught to record is lost when its holder is gone.

      Training principle: Each sector not only receives data from the DSCFM; learn to generate your own data, interpret it and build your own protocols. The DSCFM teaches the method; the sector applies it to its reality.

      10. Transversal design criteria

      These criteria guide institutional planning and must be translated into procedures, responsibilities and evaluation mechanisms.

      10.1 The climate does not affect everyone equally

      A heat wave kills more in a neighborhood without air conditioning or running water than in a corporate office.

      Operational implication: Cross-reference climate data with socioeconomic vulnerability maps. Prioritize alerts and training to those with the least capacity to respond.

      10.2 An alert without action is useless

      There is no point in predicting a flood 72 hours in advance if the community does not know where to evacuate.

      Operational implication: Each alert includes a specific action protocol. And the community is taught how to execute it.

      10.3 Scientific data must be translated

      An aerospace engineer and a small farmer need the same information, but in different formats.

      Operational implication: Communication and technical translation team. Multiple simultaneous formats.

      10.4 Prevention as a management component

      Preventative measures can improve preparedness; Its costs and results must be evaluated for each context.

      Operational implication: The budget requires validation and its results must be evaluated with indicators, baseline and independent review.

      10.5 Nobody is left out

      Climate change impacts everything from phytoplankton to satellites. Ignoring one link weakens the entire chain.

      Operational implication: Universal and transversal scope. No sector excluded from monitoring or training.

      10.6 Technology without inclusion generates inequality

      An alert app is useless for those who do not have a smartphone or electricity.

      Operational implication: Multiple simultaneous channels: digital, radio, in-person, community, SMS. Always a backup analog channel.

      10.7 The historical record is not a luxury

      Without data from the past, one cannot prove that the present is abnormal or project the future.

      Operational implication: Historical reconstruction unit. Minimum 50 years of data. Includes archives, agricultural, health, journalistic records and oral histories.

      10.8 Data without temporal continuity are useless

      An isolated measurement is an anecdote. A series of 30 years is evidence.

      Operational implication: Permanent institutional rank, protected by law. Guaranteed budget. Stable staff.

      10.9 The human species is part of the ecosystem

      Local communities are the first affected and the first to detect changes. Its empirical knowledge is data as valid as that of a sensor.

      Operational implication: Incorporate local knowledge and citizen science as legitimate sources. Community observers are an integral part of the network.

      10.10 Opportunity is also part of monitoring

      A division that only registers problems generates paralysis. One that also identifies opportunities generates action.

      Operational implication: Each report includes a "Detected Opportunities" section.

      10.11 Teaching how to think is more transformative than providing answers

      If the DSCFM only delivers finished protocols, it creates dependency. If you teach how to build a protocol from observation and recording, you create autonomy. And autonomy is the only true adaptation.

      Operational implication: Each DSCFM product includes a “How was this built?” The protocol generation methodology is public, open and replicable. The final objective of the DSCFM is not to be indispensable: it is that each organization can carry out its own monitoring without needing it permanently.

      11. DAILY REGISTRATION SYSTEM AND PROTOCOLS GENERATED BY THE DIVISION

      11.1 Principle: protocols are born from the thinking of the Division

      The DSCFM does not import generic protocols from international organizations to apply them without adaptation. Generate your own protocols from the following process:

      1. Observation and daily recording of phenomena.
      2. Data crossing between climatic, biological, health and productive variables.
      3. Identification of patterns and correlations that were not visible before the crossing.
      4. Formulation of hypotheses on causes, trends and projections.
      5. Validation of the hypothesis with additional data or with the record of the following weeks/months.
      6. Protocol writing: concrete procedure, step by step, adapted to the local context, with those responsible, times and resources.
      7. Protocol teaching: It is not delivered as a closed prescription; The logic that generated it is taught so that each recipient can adapt it.
      8. Check and adjustment: the protocol is updated with new data. It is not static.

      This process is the core methodology of DSCFM and is what is taught.

      11.4 Data loading and validation protocol

      1. The operator completes the form in the field (digital or paper).
      2. Load on the central platform within 24 hours.
      3. The system validates completeness, coherence and georeferencing.
      4. The data analyst reviews and confirms the upload.
      5. The data is integrated into the corresponding time series.
      6. If a significant anomaly is detected, the analysis protocol is activated.
      7. The data can be cross-referenced with historical time series to support preliminary analyses, once the thresholds and procedures have been defined and validated.

      11.5 Network of community observers

      • 20 to 50 people trained by the DSCFM.
      • Trained in the method: observe → record → report.
      • They are not "auxiliary": they are an integral part of the monitoring network.
      • They receive quarterly refresher training.
      • They provide local knowledge and empirical observations.
      • Over time, they generate their own autonomous mini-records.
      • Incentives: University certification (agreement with national universities), field equipment, annual public recognition, priority access to alerts.

      11.6 Methodology for generating own protocols

      The DSCFM documents and publishes its protocol generation methodology so that it is replicable:

      Step 1 – Observation: What am I seeing? Precise description of the phenomenon. Step 2 – Registration: What data do I have? Frequency, magnitude, location, evidence. Step 3 – Crossover: What other data is related? Climatic, biological, health, and productive variables. Step 4 – Thinking: What pattern emerges? What hypothesis can I formulate? Step 5 – Projection: What will happen if the trend continues? In what period? Step 6 – Protocol: What should be done, who does it, when, with what resources? Step 7 – Teaching: How do I explain the logic of this protocol for others to adapt? Step 8 – Review: Did the protocol work? What new data confirms or denies it?

      • Health and environment: study associations between meteorological conditions, health surveillance and territorial variables, under the supervision of health authorities.

      12.3 Minimum conditions for developing models

      1. Define a specific question, the user of the product and the decision it could support.
      2. Inventory sources, permits, coverage, quality, comparable periods, and missing data; Do not assume that access is guaranteed.
      3. Establish a baseline and temporally separate development and evaluation data.
      4. Compare the model with a relevant reference and report error, uncertainty, biases and limits of use.
      5. Perform independent validation with specialists and users before issuing operational products.
      6. Monitor performance and suspend or revise the product when evidence or conditions change.

      Communication rule: Do not publish precision, anticipation, or damage reduction figures until performance is measured with documented data and a reproducible method. The alerts and recommendations correspond to the competent sectoral authorities.

      18.9 Method for evaluating costs and results

      Evaluation principles

      1. Define the question and scope: specify the event, territory, population and period analyzed.
      2. Consolidate sources: record origin, cut-off date, unit, coverage, methodology and limitations of each data set.

      18.11 Follow-up exercises in Argentine cases

      Content type: hypothetical retrospective planning exercises. They do not reconstruct alerts that were actually issued nor do they allow us to affirm what damage would have been avoided. Historical records, dates and results must be cross-checked with official sources before being presented as facts.

      Exercise 1: dengue surveillance and environmental conditions

      Job question: What use would it be to integrate health records, entomological surveillance and meteorological variables with sufficient coverage and temporal resolution?

      1. Prepare the baseline: gather official data on cases, vector surveillance and meteorology; record coverage, dates, definitions and missing data.
      2. Design the analysis: define in advance territory, time window, indicators and alert criteria, with review by health authorities.
      3. Rehearse retrospectively: apply the criteria to past periods without using future information and measure anticipation, successes, false alarms and undetected events.
      4. Define possible answers: evaluate preventive actions with the competent authorities, without assuming that an environmental signal alone justifies fumigation or other intervention.
      5. Communicate limits: report uncertainty and do not convert hypothetical differences into avoided cases, deaths or savings.

      Exercise 2: drought, production and river logistics

      Job question: How can we link climate, hydrological and sectoral information to anticipate production, transportation and energy decisions without presenting a prediction as certainty?

      1. Retrieve the information available on each date: archive published forecasts and observations, with their date of issue to avoid retrospective bias.
      2. Specify scenarios: describe plausible ranges of water availability and their implications for each sector, indicating assumptions and responsible parties.
      3. Consult the actors: document which options were technically, economically and legally viable at that time.
      4. Evaluate performance: compare the alert with observations and record what decisions were made, without attributing loss changes to a hypothetical action.

      Use of both exercises: They serve to design a pilot, prioritize data and agree on inter-institutional protocols. Any economic estimate would require a published methodology, validated costs and independent review, in accordance with Annex 18.9.

      19. Conclusion and next steps

      The DSCFM proposal proposes a coordination function to improve continuous observation, the integration of information and the translation of data into decisions. Its value will depend on clear governance, agreements with competent bodies, data quality, response protocols and transparent evaluation.

      Conditions for responsible implementation

      • Specify mandate, territorial scope and responsibilities to avoid duplicating existing functions.
      • Carry out institutional, technical and budgetary pre-feasibility with verifiable costs.
      • Start with a limited pilot, published indicators and local coordination mechanisms.
      • Separate observations, forecasts, alerts and hypothetical scenarios; record uncertainties and revisions.
      • Independently evaluate results before attributing impacts or communicating economic benefits.
      • Publish data and methodology respecting privacy, sectoral competencies and quality criteria.

      The Argentine cases included in this document motivate follow-up and prevention questions. They do not in themselves prove that a specific intervention would have prevented its consequences. Retrospective exercises are presented as work scenarios to design future evaluations, not as savings obtained nor as results attributable to an institution not yet implemented.

      Proposed decision: advance to a pre-feasibility and pilot design stage, with the participation of the responsible institutions and independent technical review of sources, costs, indicators and protocols.
